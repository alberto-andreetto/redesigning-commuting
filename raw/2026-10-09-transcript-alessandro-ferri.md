[00:01:31] Interviewer: How long have you been doing it?

[00:02:02] Interviewer: Walk me through a normal morning.

[00:02:08] Alessandro: 06:40 out of the door, at the station by five to seven. The 07:05. I wait — ten minutes, twelve, standing in front of the board, that's just where you stand at Bergamo. Coffee from the machine, the real bar is too slow. Then, the front car. I'll tell you the trick.

[00:02:51] Alessandro: By Treviglio it's full. But nobody walks to the very front — they come up the stairs and they stop, herd behavior. So: front car, first doors, I sit. Every time. Three days a week I sit while the corridor fills. It's a small thing. But forty minutes standing you arrive already half-tired; sitting, the ride is mine.

[00:03:40] Interviewer: What about the mornings when you don't sit?

[00:03:47] Alessandro: When they change the stock last minute — the train comes in short, two cars missing, and the whole platform does the math at the same second. Then it's a lottery, and I'm fine at the pole, I'm not precious. But the ride is different. Standing, fifty minutes is just endurance. You do email one-handed. There's no thinking.

[00:04:44] Interviewer: You said the ride is "yours" — what do you do with it?

[00:04:52] Alessandro: I download everything at home. After Treviglio the signal is theoretical. So — podcasts at breakfast, the news, the emails I pretend I will answer on the train. And some of them I do answer. Honestly? The days I sit, I like the ride. There. I said it. It's the only guaranteed thinking time of my day. No one can call you — no, they can, but the signal is theoretical. [laughs]

[00:05:58] Interviewer: And waiting — at Bergamo, at Centrale?

[00:06:05] Alessandro: Bergamo in the morning, ten minutes, it's nothing, it's coffee and the board. The real waiting is coming home. The 18:10 out of Centrale, and I'm on the platform twenty minutes before it, because I'd rather wait there than leave the office later. That wait I don't mind at all. It's decompression. Work is still in my head on the platform; by Bergamo it's gone. The phone, mostly. Some people-watching. Centrale in the evening is a theater.

[00:07:15] Interviewer: Let's talk about delays. How do you find out when something goes wrong?

[00:07:24] Alessandro: The board. Always the board first, at Bergamo — because you're standing in front of it anyway. The app I check after, and I'll tell you why I don't trust it. The app says five minutes late, the board says five minutes late, and the train arrives fifteen minutes late. They tell you the number they hope. Every time. So I stopped consulting it like an oracle.

[00:08:05] Interviewer: So what do you trust?

[00:08:10] Alessandro: The regulars. There's a gentleman — must be sixty-five — he's done it forever, he knows the drivers by name. If he's on the platform and he's relaxed, I'm relaxed. The platform is the real app. Someone always asks him, and the answer travels down the platform like — you can watch it move. That's the announcement system. [laughs]

[00:08:55] Interviewer: Tell me about the last time something went really wrong.

[00:09:02] Alessandro: [pause] Thursday, two weeks ago. Held outside Treviglio. Twenty-five minutes, maybe thirty. One announcement — "technical problem at Treviglio" — in Italian only, and half that car was commuting to Milan and not all of them are Italian, so a lot of nothing for a lot of people. Then silence. I was fine — I had no morning meeting, I was doing my podcast, honestly it cost me nothing. But there was a girl near me on the phone to her boss, apologizing. Twice. The same delay is not the same delay for everyone. I stood there thinking — this is why I can afford to be philosophical. I'm the lucky one on this train.

[00:10:31] Interviewer: What did you do during those thirty minutes?

[00:10:38] Alessandro: Nothing new. The podcast was already going. I watched the girl, I watched the Treviglio people doing the math about the doors — when you stop at a station, people line up at the doors like it means something. When we moved again, half of them fell over. [laughs] No — I shouldn't laugh. But you asked what I did, and the honest answer is: I was fine, and I watched other people not be fine.

[00:11:35] Interviewer: If you could change one thing about how that morning worked —

[00:11:44] Alessandro: Tell me before I leave the house. That's it. If the line is sick in the morning, tell me at 06:30, and it's a choice — take the 07:05 anyway, or the 07:35, or work from home and take the 09:15 empty. Instead you find out at Treviglio, standing, when the choice is already gone. The information exists. It's just — later than the decision.

[00:12:40] Interviewer: And the delay itself — how would you want it told?
