# Desk research — How commuters get delay information today

The information chain behind research question 2: how a Milan rail commuter learns that something changed — official operator apps, third-party trackers, departure boards, word of mouth — and where trust in that chain breaks down. Collected during the Lesson 02 hands-on; every claim traces to a numbered source, and anything not yet verified is marked as pending — never dropped.

## The official layer: what the operator promises

Trenord, the operator running the regional trains through Milano Centrale, promises real-time information in its own app: "info on timetables, routes, delays, suppressions and announcements on all trains in real time", plus "personalised travel information" and "real-time notifications on your train" [S1]. Two further official channels extend the promise: a WhatsApp service called "My Train", which lets a commuter track one train and "receive free notifications and updates about your journey before, during, and after" [S2], and a trip-notification feature that sends an update on the selected train "10 minutes before boarding" [S6]. Grade: operator statements about its own products — reliable as *promises*, untested as performance.

## The third-party layer: what commuters open instead

Alongside the official app sits a third-party ecosystem. Trenìt!, self-described as "the most popular app to search trains schedules and prices in Italy" [S3], searches across operators — Trenitalia, Italo, Trenord and others — and offers "detailed infos about trains status, with the possibility of receiving notifications" [S3]. It is older than the current official apps' features: first released in September 2009 as "Italian Trains Timetable", renamed Trenìt! in May 2014 [S3]. Official data also flows through ViaggiaTreno, Trenitalia's real-time service for "information in tempo reale sull'andamento dei treni" and on departures and arrivals at the stations it serves [S4]. That the third-party layer aggregates multiple operators while each official app covers only its own is a structural fact of the chain — the commuter's information is scattered by design.

## The physical layer: departure boards and announcements

Station departure boards remain the shared, device-free channel — the one every commuter can read at Milano Centrale regardless of which app they use. How the boards behave during platform changes and disruptions — how fast they update, how legible the cause is — is not documented by any source collected here: **pending verification**, to be observed directly in the two scheduled observation sessions at Milano Centrale (2026-10-09 morning peak; 2026-10-13 delay episode; no observation note committed yet).

## The social layer: word of mouth

Asking other commuters on the platform, colleagues, group chats — no verifiable desk source for how this layer works or how fast it travels. **Pending verification**; the interviews scheduled for 2026-10-08 → 10 and the observations are the collection points for it.

## Where the chain breaks

The desk evidence for breakdown is indirect but consistent. First, the official layer fails on its own promises at least sometimes: an App Store reviewer reports that "a month after introducing a new ticketing system, customers still cannot buy tickets via the app" [S5] — a single user review (tertiary), but a documented case of the official channel breaking in public view. Second, the sheer existence and popularity of the third-party layer [S3] indicates the official apps alone are not enough for many commuters — a tracker that covers all operators should not be necessary if each operator's app were sufficient. Third, the promises themselves expose the gap: a notification "10 minutes before boarding" [S6] is an update *at the platform*, not an answer to the commuter's earlier question — *should I leave home yet, and on which train?* No source collected here documents when a delay first becomes visible in any channel relative to when the commuter needs it: **pending verification**. No source documents cancelled trains still appearing as scheduled, late platform changes, or drifting delay estimates: **pending verification** — all three are recurring commuter claims that the interviews and observations are scheduled to confirm or refute.

## Signals — reading, not evidence

For Brief 1 (research question 2):

- **Information scattered across apps** — the brief's pain point is structural: each official app covers one operator; only third-party trackers aggregate; the commuter assembles the truth from multiple channels. A feature that reduces the assembly work addresses the structure, not just one app's shortcomings.
- **Uncertainty about arrival times** — the official promises are about *notifying* ("receive notifications"), not about *explaining* (cause, magnitude, what to do). The promise language itself suggests where the anxiety gap sits.
- **Trust is layered, not total** — official channels promise, third parties aggregate, boards confirm, people verify. No single channel is described as sufficient on its own; the chain works only when the commuter does the cross-checking. Where trust breaks is where the cross-checking cost becomes too high — the point the interviews should locate.
- The pendings above are the desk note's honest edge: the breakdown claims commuters actually make (cancelled trains still shown, late platform changes, drifting estimates) are unverified from the desk and must be confirmed by the primary research before being used in synthesis.
