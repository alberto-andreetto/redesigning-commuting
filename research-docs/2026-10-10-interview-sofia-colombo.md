# Interview — Sofia Colombo, off-peak shift commuter

Interviewed at Milano Centrale on a Saturday afternoon after her shift, twenty-five minutes, recorded with consent. Verbatim transcript: `raw/2026-10-10-transcript-sofia-colombo.md`; designer's observations from the session: `raw/2026-10-10-fieldnotes-sofia-colombo.md`. Quotes below reference the transcript timestamps.

## Who she is

Twenty-seven, retail shift worker at a shop near the Duomo. Lives in Lodi: regional train to Milano Centrale (~half an hour), then the M3 to Duomo. The shift schedule is published two weeks ahead and rotates, Sundays included — so, in her words, "you don't really have 'a commute', you have a new commute every day, because the shift decides the train" [00:01:34].

## The plan is made the night before, and it lives in three places

The night before every shift she opens the operator app, picks the train that gets her in twenty minutes early, and screenshots it — time, train number, platform if already known, plus the two following trains "in case" [00:02:11]. The twenty-minute buffer is deliberate: "because ten is hope. Twenty covers — the train being late, the metro, the line at the shop door before opening" [00:03:04]. The tool chain is three apps and an artifact:

> "The Trenord one for the trains, Google Maps for the metro leg, the screenshot for — the truth, I guess. The plan lives in three places: the app, the picture, and my head. And my head is the only one that checks all three." — [00:08:41]

## Off-peak is quiet, and it forgives nothing

Off-peak trains are empty — "I sit every time, even mid-morning" — but the service is thin, and on Sundays some trains do not run at all. Her summary:

> "In the morning peak there's another train six minutes behind yours. Off-peak, Sunday off-peak, there's nothing behind you. One every half hour, and some that just don't run on Sunday. Off-peak forgives nothing." — [00:03:55]

## When the plan breaks: the Sunday that was not there

Three weeks before the interview, a Sunday 09:30 shift: the 08:52 from Lodi, planned "to the minute". At the platform at 08:40, the train was not on the board — "not delayed. Not there. Canceled doesn't even show" — while the app still showed it running [00:05:02]. The station info point confirmed the cancellation, and explained the app's behavior, in one sentence she repeated twice:

> "It was canceled this morning, the app doesn't always update on Sundays. The app doesn't always update on Sundays. That's — that's the sentence. That's the whole thing. The next one was 09:32. Forty minutes on the platform." — [00:06:04]

The app corrected the 08:52 to canceled at about 09:10 — after the train was already gone [00:07:56]. She called her manager from the platform, first time in two years, and arrived fifteen minutes late to a half-opened shop:

> "The fifteen minutes is nothing. Everyone seeing you arrive late is the thing." — [00:06:58]

## The platform becomes the announcement

During the forty-minute wait, the platform organized itself around the failure — the same word-of-mouth layer Alessandro Ferri described at Bergamo, described here independently:

> "The man who travels with the dog, he went first to ask, he came back and told everyone. Then we all told the next arrivals. By 09:00 everyone on that platform knew the train was dead, except the app in everyone's hand. We were the announcement." — [00:11:48]

Alternatives were arithmetic, not options: the Sunday bus would have arrived "at lunch"; the taxi "half a day's pay" [00:12:35].

## What she wants is to be told her train died

Her ask is not a better app in general — it is a message about *her* train, before *her* decision moment:

> "The app knows my train. It knows the number, it knows I search it the night before, it knows I search it again in the morning. Why do I have to ask it three times? It should tell me. One message, at night or when I wake up: 'Your 08:52 doesn't exist tomorrow.' And then I replan at home, with coffee — not on the platform, with my manager's name in my hand." — [00:13:21]

## Signals — reading, not evidence

Mapping to the brief's pain points:

- **Information scattered across apps** — the strongest structural signal of the three interviews: the plan lives in three places (app, screenshot, head) and the cross-checking is done by the commuter, in her head, twice a day. Confirms the desk picture in `research-docs/2026-10-05-desk-delay-information.md`.
- **Delays and unexpected changes** — for a shift commuter the lethal failure is the *cancellation*, not the delay: a delay costs minutes, a cancellation costs the only train within forty minutes. Off-peak has no recovery train behind it.
- **Uncertainty about arrival times** — her defense is self-built: the twenty-minute buffer, the screenshot, the two backup trains. The anxiety is not eliminated, it is *maintained* — every night, by hand.
- **Recovering from a delay** — the cost of the Sunday was social, not temporal: arriving visibly late, in front of colleagues. The same delay is not the same delay — Ferri said it watching a stranger apologize to a boss; Sofia was the stranger.
- **Word of mouth** — second independent primary account of the platform working as the announcement system when the official channels fail. Two of three commuters now describe this layer; the desk note's pending "social layer" has primary evidence.
- Sofia's Sunday is also primary evidence for the desk note's pending claim that cancelled trains can still appear as scheduled in the official app — one documented case, one line, one day of the week. One case is not a rate.

Strongest candidate moment from this interview: **the night-before notification** — "tell me when my train dies," before the plan is locked. The same shape as Ferri's "morning before the morning": information delivered before the leave-home decision.
